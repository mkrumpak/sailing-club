The Corner Effect and the Heineken Regatta's Around the Island Race

Thirty miles, one island, and a course that never lets the wind stay consistent for long. The Around the Island Race at the St. Maarten Heineken Regatta is the closest thing sailing has to a live physics lesson.

Most of that lesson comes down to what happens where land meets moving air. Sailors call it the Corner Effect: the way wind bends, accelerates, and sometimes disappears entirely as it wraps around a headland. Know where the corners are on this course, and you're not just racing the fleet — you're racing the terrain.

What the Corner Effect Actually Is

Over open water, wind flows across a smooth, low-friction surface, so it stays relatively steady in both speed and direction. Land changes that. A headland is a physical barrier the airflow must get past, and it does so in two ways: some of the air rises up and over the terrain, and some is deflected sideways around it. How the flow divides depends on the height of the land and the stability of the air. Stable air resists rising and tends to go around, while less stable air lifts over more readily.

The air forced over the top leaves a wind shadow on the downwind side: a zone of reduced, gusty, and unpredictable breeze that can extend several times the height of the land downwind. The air deflected around the side is squeezed into a narrower path as the streamlines bunch together at the point, and to keep the same volume of air moving through that tighter space, it has to speed up. At the same time, the wind bends to follow the shape of the coastline.

That combination of acceleration and bending is the Corner Effect. It works much like water flowing past a bridge pier or a rock in a stream: the flow is fastest where it squeezes past the edge of the obstacle, and it curves to follow the obstacle's outline. For a sailor, this means that approaching the right headland at the right angle will usually bring stronger pressure and a favorable shift, so you're lifted as you close in on the point. Stray too far past it into the lee, though, and you drop into the shadow zone, where the same breeze can become light, shifty, or disappear almost entirely.

Pointe Blanche: Hug the Cliff or Pay for It

There's no better place to see this than Pointe Blanche, the cliffs on Sint Maarten's southeastern tip that every boat in the Around the Island Race has to round. At this year's 46th running of the race, the fleet worked down the coast and swept around the cliffs at Pointe Blanche on the way past the Port of St. Maarten — one of the course's defining moments, and one of its most tactical.

The trade winds hit those cliffs directly, and because the air can't easily climb over, it compresses and wraps hard around the point. Local knowledge says to hug the cliffs here rather than stand off — sail into that compression zone and the wrapping breeze gives a genuine, usable boost in pressure, the kind of margin that turns boats rounding a headland together into boats several lengths apart on the other side.

The Hole Behind Philipsburg: Little Bay's Wind Shadow

Round the southern tip of the island and head downwind past Philipsburg toward Little Bay, and the same terrain that just gave you a boost starts taking it away. The high ground blocks the easterly trades here, leaving a fragmented, turbulent breeze at the surface instead of the clean pressure you had a few miles back.

This is the flip side of the Corner Effect, and it catches sailors who only planned for the acceleration half of the lesson. The shortest course line often runs straight through the worst of the shadow — which means the tactical call isn't just about distance, it's about balancing the shortest route against staying far enough offshore to hold onto usable breeze.

The Anguilla Channel: The Course's Last Compression Zone

By the time the course turns for the island's western end and works back upwind, boats run into a different version of the same effect — this time on a bigger scale. The trade winds funnel between the landmasses of Saint Martin and Anguilla, compressing the same way they do around a single headland, just across a much wider gap.

The Anguilla Channel is one of the most reliable acceleration zones anywhere near Saint Martin, adding pressure — and steeper, choppier seas — on top of whatever the open water was already giving you. Crews who've felt it coming know to shift gears early, easing the traveler and adding twist before the gust arrives, not after.

This Year's Reminder Not to Get Greedy

The 2026 Around the Island Race made the risk side of all this hard to miss. Conditions held in the high teens and gusted past 20 knots most of the day, and the power around the island's corners caught more than a few boats out. That's the real lesson underneath the Corner Effect. The acceleration around a headland is useful, right up until it's more breeze than your sail plan can handle. The sailors who do well on this course aren't the ones who ignore the corners, and they're not the ones who play it safe around every one of them either — they're the ones who know which corner rewards getting close and which one is about to ask their rig a question it can't answer.

Ready to Read a Coastline Like a Tactician?

Reading terrain like this takes time on the water around it — watching how the wind behaves at Pointe Blanche, past Little Bay, and through the Anguilla Channel, not just hearing about it secondhand.
