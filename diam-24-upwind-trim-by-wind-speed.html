Diam 24 Upwind Trim, Band by Band: How the Whole Setup Changes With the Wind

Look at where the mast rotates on a Diam 24 across the wind range and you'd think you were looking at three different boats: forward of the side stay in five knots, facing the apparent wind at ten, pulled back almost onto the boom by twenty.

Upwind trim on this boat isn't one setup you dial in and leave. It's a full progression, and knowing where you are in it — not just what the wind meter reads — is most of what fast trim actually comes down to.

Underpowered: Chasing Power, Not Controlling It (3–8 Knots)

Below about 8 knots, every setting on the boat is chasing the same goal: generate power, don't waste any of it. The foot of the main stays very open, the sail carries a twisted shape rather than a firm one, and the traveler sits well up — around 20cm above centerline — to keep the boom from choking off flow in air that's already scarce.

Crew position follows the same logic from an unexpected direction: weight goes forward, with the lead crew out on the leeward floater rather than anywhere near the wing. In light air there's no heeling force to counteract, so the priority shifts to bow-down trim and keeping wetted surface working for you, not fighting a heel angle that barely exists yet.

The Turning Point: 9 to 11 Knots

This band is labeled the end of the underpowered range for a reason — nearly every setting on the boat pivots here at once, not gradually. Mast rotation swings from sitting forward of the side stay to facing the apparent wind directly. The traveler comes down from +20cm to dead center. The mainsheet firms up. And crew position flips: the second crew and the helm move from forward and leeward to windward, the first real shift toward hiking.

It's not a coincidence that this band lines up closely with where the boat starts flying its main hull clear of the water. The tuning changes aren't incidental to that transition — they're what makes it happen cleanly instead of getting fought the whole way through.

Full Power: Hiking and Holding On (12–15 Knots)

By 12 knots you're in the boat's sweet spot — full power, fast, and everything trimmed to hold that rather than build more of it. Real hiking starts here, with crew weight moving out past the side stay. The mainsheet comes on hard, the jib clew drops down a hole to flatten the sail, and the foot starts closing in from wide-open to a more moderate shape.

This is the band where the boat rewards precision most. Power is no longer scarce, so the job shifts from generating it to using exactly the right amount — enough to stay fast, not so much that you're fighting the helm for the next four knots of breeze.

Overpowered: Everything Runs in Reverse (16–26 Knots)

Past about 16 knots, the entire progression from the first three bands effectively reverses. Rig tension keeps climbing even as everything else backs off — side stay load that starts around 80kg in the lightest air is already past 500kg here, on its way to roughly 650kg at the top of the range. Mast rotation, which spent the light-air range swinging toward the apparent wind, now retreats — back past the side stay and eventually in close to the boom. The traveler keeps dropping, down to -30cm and beyond, and the foot goes flat.

By 19 to 22 knots the guide calls for reefing the main outright, with settings adjusting again around the smaller sail plan. Past 23, it's labeled survival range — the goal isn't speed anymore, it's keeping the boat manageable and the rig intact.

Ready to Feel Where You Are in the Range?

Numbers on a page tell you what the settings are. Time on the water with a coach is what teaches you to feel which band you're actually in before your tuning falls behind the breeze.
