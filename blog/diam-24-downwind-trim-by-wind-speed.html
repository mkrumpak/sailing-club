Diam 24 Downwind Trim, Band by Band: Reading the Leeward Float

How do you know if you're pressing the leeward float exactly the right amount downwind on a Diam 24? The class tuning guide has a surprisingly literal answer: watch the waterline, and don't let it climb past two-thirds of the hull.

That's the kind of concrete, visual reference point that turns abstract trim advice into something you can see happening in real time — and it's just one piece of a downwind setup that changes almost completely between light air and a strong breeze.

Light Air: Heat It Up, Without Overcooking It (3–8 Knots)

In the lightest air, the job is building and keeping apparent wind. Heat the boat up, sailing a little higher to build speed, and bear away only as far as the speed holds. Go too deep and the boat stalls; go too high and you sail extra distance for speed you didn't need. The target angle on your polar diagram is the one that balances the two, and that's the number to steer to (see our post on reading a Diam 24 polar).

Crew stays forward and on the leeward float, with the second crew near the forestay and the helm working from the leeward float as well in the very lightest air before moving back into the cockpit as breeze fills in. The cunningham stays loose and the jib stays rolled away — nothing here is about power yet. It's about finding the angle that keeps the boat moving rather than parked.

The Transition: Reading the Leeward Float (9–11 Knots)

This band introduces one of the more useful, specific pieces of guidance in the whole tuning guide: you can press the leeward float until the waterline climbs to about two-thirds of the hull's height, and that waterline becomes a reference point for managing trim rather than guessing from feel alone. It's a small thing, but it turns an abstract instruction — don't overpower the leeward float — into something you can watch happen in real time.

Crew position holds close to where it was in lighter air, still forward and on the leeward float, with the helm now settled into the cockpit. This band is really about calibration: using that visual reference to hold the boat right at the edge of pressing the float hard without burying it.

Full Power: High Mode (12–15 Knots)

By 12 knots the tuning notes shift to a single, direct instruction: high mode, fast. This is the band where downwind trim stops being about finding speed and starts being about holding a genuinely quick mode without giving any of it back.

Crew position adjusts accordingly — the second crew moves back into the cockpit, while the lead crew stays out on the leeward float. The cunningham is still loose here; the boat isn't overpowered yet, it's just properly powered up and moving well.

Overpowered: Ease, Watch the Bow, Firm Up (16–26 Knots)

Past 16 knots, the whole downwind picture shifts toward control. The clearest instruction in the entire guide shows up here: the trimmer needs to prepare to ease in the gusts, full stop. Crew moves further aft and outboard, and by the top of the range both are positioned as far aft as the boat allows.

The cunningham and mainsheet tell the same story from different angles. The cunningham stays loose through the whole lighter range, then firms up at 16 to 18 knots and goes fully on hard by 23 to 26. On the mainsheet, ease as each gust loads the sail, let the boat accelerate, then trim back in as the apparent wind moves forward. Hold a well-twisted shape and watch for nosediving. A leeward bow that starts to bury is the real hazard downwind in breeze, which is exactly why easing early and firmly matters more here than anywhere else in this wind range.

Ready to Read Your Own Waterline?

A reference point like "two-thirds up the hull" only becomes useful once you've watched it happen a few times, ideally with someone confirming what you're seeing is right.
