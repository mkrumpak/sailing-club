Target Speed, Target Angle: How to Read a Diam 24 Polar

Walk past a serious Diam 24 campaign and you'll often find a small, laminated card taped right where the trimmer can glance at it mid-race — rows of numbers by wind speed and angle, worn soft at the corners from being checked a hundred times a regatta.

That card is the polar, reduced to exactly what a crew needs in the moment: what speed should we be doing right now, at this angle, in this breeze?

What a Polar Actually Tells You

A polar is deceptively simple: for a given wind speed, it tells you the boat speed you should be able to hold at any given angle to the wind. Look at the Diam 24's numbers in 10 knots of true wind, sailing upwind — 9.0 knots of boat speed at a tight 40 degrees, climbing to 13.0 knots by the time you open up to 70 or 80 degrees. Every one of those pairings is a target: a specific speed the boat should be making at that specific angle, in that specific breeze.

The point isn't to hit the highest number on the chart. It's to hit the right number for the angle you're actually trying to sail — because the fastest boat speed and the fastest way to your next mark are usually two different angles entirely.

Why the Target Angle Changes With the Wind

Look down the Diam 24's upwind numbers and the target angle isn't fixed — it shifts as the breeze builds. In 6 knots, boat speed climbs from 5.5 at 40 degrees up to 7.5 by 70 degrees, then simply holds there through 80 and 90 — past that point, opening the angle further buys you nothing. In 18 knots, there's no plateau at all: speed keeps climbing the whole way, from 13.5 at 40 degrees to a full 20 knots at 90.

That's apparent wind doing what it always does on a fast boat: as boat speed increases with the breeze, the wind you're actually sailing to shifts forward and strengthens, which changes where the best trade-off between speed and angle actually sits. A target angle borrowed from a different wind speed is really a target for a different boat — the numbers only work in the band they were built for.

Reading the Shape: Upwind vs. Downwind

Downwind tells an even more direct version of the same story. At 6 knots, boat speed drops steadily as the angle opens up — 7.0 knots at 100 degrees down to 4.5 knots at 155 degrees. Sailing deep in light air doesn't just feel slow, the polar confirms it costs you real speed for very little extra distance made good. That's the number behind "heat it up" as a piece of advice: in light air, a tighter angle is faster in absolute terms, not just a feel-based preference.

By 14 and 18 knots downwind, that gap mostly closes. At 18 knots, boat speed barely moves at all — holding between 19 and 19.5 knots across the entire range from 100 to 145 degrees — and 14 knots stays within a knot of its own peak across the same span. In more breeze, angle matters far less for raw speed, which shifts the decision toward whatever angle actually gets you to the next mark fastest, since you're not sacrificing much boat speed either way.

Using the Numbers on the Water

None of this matters if the numbers stay on a laminated card nobody looks at. The reason serious crews tape a polar where the trimmer can see it mid-race is simple: it turns "we feel slow" into something specific. If the wind's reading 14 knots and you're sailing 60 degrees but the boat's only doing 14 knots instead of the 16 the polar says you should be hitting, that's not a vague feeling — it's a two-knot gap with a cause, whether that's trim, a sea state the polar doesn't account for, or dirty air from another boat.

Used well, a target isn't a scoreboard. It's a diagnostic tool — a number that tells you the moment something's off, well before you'd notice it any other way.

Ready to Sail to Your Own Numbers?

Reading a polar is one thing. Building the instinct to check your speed against a target without even glancing at the card — because you already know roughly where you should be — takes real time on the water with someone confirming when you're actually on target and when you're not.
