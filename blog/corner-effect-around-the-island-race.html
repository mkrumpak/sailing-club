The Corner Effect and the Heineken Regatta's Around the Island Race

Thirty miles, one island, and a course that never lets the wind stay simple for long. The Around the Island Race at the St. Maarten Heineken Regatta is the closest thing sailing has to a live physics lesson.

Most of that lesson comes down to what happens where land meets moving air. Sailors call it the Corner Effect: the way wind bends, accelerates, and sometimes disappears entirely as it wraps around a headland. Know where the corners are on this course, and you're not just racing the fleet — you're racing the terrain.

What the Corner Effect Actually Is

Wind moving over open water is relatively undisturbed — smooth, predictable, easy to trim to. The moment that same wind hits a landmass, it has to go somewhere. Some of it lifts up and over, creating turbulence and a wind shadow on the far side. The rest bends around the edges.

That bending is the Corner Effect. Because land creates more friction than water, wind converging off a headland speeds up and shifts direction as it wraps around the point — similar to the way water speeds up around a bend in a river. Get close to the right headland at the right angle, and the breeze will typically build and lift you as you approach. Get pulled too far into the shadow just past it, and that same wind can turn light, shifty, or disappear almost entirely.

Pointe Blanche: Hug the Cliff or Pay for It

There's no better place to see this than Pointe Blanche, the cliffs on Sint Maarten's southeastern tip that every boat in the Around the Island Race has to round. At this year's 46th running of the race, the fleet worked down the coast and swept around the cliffs at Pointe Blanche on the way past the Port of St. Maarten — one of the course's defining moments, and one of its most tactical.

The trade winds hit those cliffs directly, and because the air can't easily climb over, it compresses and wraps hard around the point. Local knowledge says to hug the cliffs here rather than stand off — sail into that compression zone and the wrapping breeze gives a genuine, usable boost in pressure, the kind of margin that turns boats rounding a headland together into boats several lengths apart on the other side.

The Hole Behind Philipsburg: Little Bay's Wind Shadow

Round the southern tip of the island and head downwind past Philipsburg toward Little Bay, and the same terrain that just gave you a boost starts taking it away. The high ground blocks the easterly trades here, leaving a fragmented, turbulent breeze at the surface instead of the clean pressure you had a few miles back.

This is the flip side of the Corner Effect, and it catches sailors who only planned for the acceleration half of the lesson. The shortest course line often runs straight through the worst of the shadow — which means the tactical call isn't just about distance, it's about balancing the shortest route against staying far enough offshore to hold onto usable breeze.

The Anguilla Channel: The Course's Last Compression Zone

By the time the course turns for the island's western end and works back upwind, boats run into a different version of the same effect — this time on a bigger scale. The trade winds funnel between the landmasses of Saint Martin and Anguilla, compressing the same way they do around a single headland, just across a much wider gap.

It's a stretch we've written about before: the Anguilla Channel is one of the most reliable acceleration zones anywhere near Saint Martin, adding real pressure — and steeper, choppier seas — on top of whatever the open water was already giving you. Crews who've felt it coming know to shift gears early, easing the traveler and adding twist before the gust arrives, not after.

This Year's Reminder Not to Get Greedy

The 2026 Around the Island Race made the risk side of all this hard to miss. Conditions held in the high teens and gusted past 20 knots most of the day, and the power around the island's corners caught more than a few boats out — Morpheus fought to control their spinnaker as the pressure built, while VANTANERA tore a mainsail partway through the course trying to hold onto full power a little too long.

That's the real lesson underneath the Corner Effect. The acceleration around a headland is genuinely useful, right up until it's more breeze than your sail plan can handle. The sailors who do well on this course aren't the ones who ignore the corners, and they're not the ones who play it safe around every one of them either — they're the ones who know which corner rewards getting close and which one is about to ask their rig a question it can't answer.

Ready to Read a Coastline Like a Tactician?

Reading terrain like this takes real time on the water around it — watching how the wind actually behaves at Pointe Blanche, past Little Bay, and through the Anguilla Channel, not just hearing about it secondhand.
